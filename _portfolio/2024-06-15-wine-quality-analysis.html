Project Overview

This project began as a UCLA Summer Sessions course project under the supervision of Prof. Chunyang Liao and was later independently expanded into a sole-authored conference paper. I designed and executed the complete research workflow, from data preparation and experimental design to analysis, visualization, and manuscript writing.

The study compares five ensemble-tree methods—Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost—on the public Vinho Verde wine-quality datasets. The aim was to build a controlled offline benchmark that evaluates predictive performance, compact feature subsets, and computational cost under one consistent protocol.

Key Outcomes

  • Benchmarked five ensemble-tree methods on 6,497 samples: 1,599 red wines and 4,898 white wines, each described by 11 physicochemical features.
  • Built a leakage-controlled evaluation pipeline with an untouched 20% test set and fold-local preprocessing within the training data.
  • Obtained the highest weighted F1 with Gradient Boosting: 0.693 ± 0.028 for red wine and 0.664 ± 0.016 for white wine.
  • Measured the weighted-F1 trade-off when each model-and-dataset combination was reduced from 11 inputs to its own five highest-ranked features.
  • Expanded the work into a sole-authored paper published at IPMLP 2025, with the proceedings published by ACM.

Leakage-Controlled Evaluation Pipeline

The combined dataset contains 11 continuous physicochemical measurements and an ordinal quality label. Observed ratings span 3–8 for red wine and 3–9 for white wine, with most samples concentrated in the middle classes.

Each dataset was divided using an 80:20 stratified train-test split. Model selection was then performed only within the training partition using five-fold StratifiedGroupKFold. Standardization, SMOTE-Tomek resampling, and inverse-frequency weighting were fitted independently inside each training fold. This design was intended to prevent preprocessing and model-selection information from leaking out of the held-out evaluation protocol.

Model Search and Feature Selection

Optuna's Tree-structured Parzen Estimator tuned each model with a fixed search budget: 200 trials for Random Forest, 120 for Gradient Boosting, 200 for XGBoost, 200 for LightGBM, and 150 for CatBoost. Weighted F1 was the sole hyperparameter-optimization objective. Macro F1, macro ROC-AUC, Matthews correlation coefficient, and Brier score were retained as complementary evaluation measures.

After full-feature tuning, each model ranked features using its native tree-based importance measure. Every model-and-dataset combination was then refitted using its own five highest-ranked features, subject to a 5% minimum importance threshold. The resulting subsets therefore vary by algorithm and dataset; they are predictive rankings within this benchmark rather than a universal or causal list of wine-quality determinants.

Results

Gradient Boosting produced the highest held-out weighted F1 on both datasets: 0.693 ± 0.028 for red wine and 0.664 ± 0.016 for white wine. When the five model-specific features replaced all 11 inputs, the median weighted F1 decreased by 2.6 percentage points for red wine and 3.0 percentage points for white wine. The ablation therefore quantifies the performance cost of reducing the number of measured variables.

Compute profiling exposed a much larger difference in optimization cost. During the full-feature phase, mean study times were 46.3 minutes for Random Forest, 58.9 minutes for CatBoost, 92.3 minutes for XGBoost, 122.5 minutes for LightGBM, and 746.2 minutes for Gradient Boosting. Within this experimental setup, Random Forest provided the strongest compute-performance compromise, whereas Gradient Boosting defined the best weighted-F1 result at substantially higher search cost.

Error Analysis

The CatBoost red-wine experiment illustrates two recurring error patterns. Its weighted F1 decreased from 0.6406 with all 11 features to 0.5895 with the selected subset. In both confusion matrices, predictions were concentrated around the common quality scores 5 and 6, while the rarer edge classes remained more difficult to identify.

The accompanying Optuna histories show that the reduced-feature search converged to a lower validation ceiling rather than simply requiring more trials. Together, these results reinforce that the compact subsets should be treated as an efficiency-oriented ablation, not as a lossless replacement for the full measurements.

Scope and Limitations

  • The evaluation uses one public dataset family from the Vinho Verde region of Portugal; performance across other regions, vintages, or laboratory protocols was not established.
  • This work is an offline comparative benchmark, not a deployed production or real-time quality-control system.
  • The project does not provide a public code repository. The paper documents the evaluation pipeline, search spaces, software versions, and random seeds needed to interpret and reproduce the reported protocol.
  • Feature-importance rankings are specific to the evaluated models and datasets and should not be interpreted as causal scientific conclusions.
